# ICP Persona 3: The Parks & Rec Coordinator
*"I manage tennis courts, soccer fields, swim programs, and three part-time staff — and I have exactly $0 in discretionary budget."*

## 👤 Demographics

- **Age range:** 28–50 (broader range; many younger staff entering the field)
- **Education:** Recreation management, kinesiology, public administration degree, or similar
- **Salary:** Median ~$54,566/year (NRPA 2023 data); municipal pay scale
- **How they got here:** Parks & rec career path; often started as program staff or coordinator assistant
- **Reports to:** Parks & Recreation Director or Department Head; ultimately to City Manager / Mayor
- **Key relationships:** City IT (for software approval), finance/procurement, other program coordinators (pools, athletics, seniors), frontline staff (part-time instructors, court monitors)
- **Industry orgs:** NRPA (National Recreation and Park Association), state recreation & park associations, USTA Parks programs

## 🌅 Day in the Life

**7:30 AM** – Arrives at city hall or rec center. Checks voicemail: three calls asking about tennis court reservations, one complaint about a broken net on Court 7.

**8:00 AM** – Logs into the legacy registration system (RecDesk, CivicRec, ActiveNet, or equivalent — often 10+ years old). Checks tennis court bookings for the week. System can't send automated reminders; makes a note to manually email today's registrants.

**9:00 AM** – Submits a maintenance request for Court 7's net. Separately emails the parks maintenance team (different department, different system).

**10:00 AM** – Tries to run a report on tennis program participation for the quarterly city council presentation. System generates a CSV that requires manual cleanup in Excel. Spends an hour on this.

**11:30 AM** – Resident calls asking how to register their child for the summer tennis clinic. Walks them through the city website registration (which is confusing and not mobile-friendly). Resident gives up; coordinator takes their info and registers them manually over the phone.

**1:00 PM** – Meeting with department head about next fiscal year's budget. Tennis program registration is down 15%. Need to show what's working. Director asks: "Can we do online booking?" Coordinator says they've been asking IT for 2 years.

**2:30 PM** – Deal with a court conflict — a church group booked the same courts as the senior tennis program through different staff members.

**4:00 PM** – Handles staff scheduling for weekend court monitor coverage. Two part-timers called out; scrambles to find coverage via group text.

**5:00 PM** – Leaves on time because city employment rules. Emails answered tomorrow.

## 😤 Pain Points (Ranked by Intensity)

### #1 — Legacy Systems That Can't Do Online Booking Properly
The city uses an outdated rec management platform (often ActiveNet, CivicRec, or RecDesk) that technically handles court reservations — but the member-facing interface is so poor that residents give up and call instead. This creates a vicious cycle: staff spend all day on the phone, which validates IT's claim that "nobody uses the online system."

*"Getting citizens engaged via the Internet and mobile devices was a challenge. Our previous system wasn't capable of allowing citizens to register online."*
— CivicPlus customer quote

### #2 — Budget and Procurement Paralysis
Every software decision goes through procurement. A new tool under ~$5K/year might need 3 approvals; over that requires an RFP process that takes 6–18 months. The coordinator knows what they need but can't just buy it. By the time a decision is made, the need has grown worse.

*"I've been trying to get a better booking system approved for two years. We keep getting told to wait for the next budget cycle."*
— Synthesized from common municipal patterns

### #3 — Equity and Public Access Expectations
Unlike private clubs, everything a public agency does is scrutinized for fairness. Courts must be accessible to all residents. Booking rules can't favor certain groups. Any fee structure faces community pushback. Technology that looks "premium" may face political resistance ("why is the city spending money on an app?").

### #4 — Multi-Sport / Multi-Program Complexity
They don't just manage tennis. They also oversee soccer leagues, swim programs, senior fitness classes, pickleball, summer camps. Any software solution has to either work across all programs or integrate cleanly — a tennis-only tool doesn't work for a recreation department.

### #5 — Staff Capacity and Turnover
Constant budget pressure means lean staffing. Part-time staff are seasonal, young, and often unfamiliar with legacy systems. Training takes time they don't have. When a part-timer leaves, institutional knowledge leaves with them.

## 🎯 Goals (What Success Looks Like)

1. **Online self-service booking** — Residents register and pay online without calling; reduces call volume significantly
2. **Participation data for city reporting** — Can show council: how many residents used tennis courts, revenue generated, demographic reach
3. **Equity-conscious access** — Fair, transparent booking rules that can be defended to community
4. **Cross-program visibility** — One system where staff can see all programs, not just tennis
5. **Reduce overtime / manual admin** — Stop processing paper forms and phone registrations after-hours
6. **Justify tennis program budget** — Show that courts are actively used and the program contributes to community health metrics

---

## 🔍 Decision Factors (Ranked)

1. **Price / Budget Compatibility** — Must fit within existing budget or be fundable through a grant (NRPA, USTA community grants). Free tiers or low-cost entry points are critical.
2. **Procurement Compatibility** — Can the vendor provide a W9, certificate of insurance, and government contract terms? Is there an existing state contract they can piggyback on?
3. **Multi-Program Capability** — Can it handle tennis AND other recreation programs? A tennis-only tool may not get approved.
4. **Ease of Use for Residents** — Public users have no training; the booking flow must be foolproof. Accessibility (ADA compliance) matters.
5. **Reporting for City** — Must generate the reports city leadership needs for council presentations and grant applications.
6. **Integration with City Systems** — Does it work with their existing payment processor, finance software, or city website?
7. **Support** — Needs real support staff; IT help desk is not available for recreation software.
8. **Features** — Adequate coverage; doesn't need to be the most feature-rich, just reliable and easy.

## 🚧 Objections (Why They Hesitate)

| Objection | Underlying Fear |
|-----------|----------------|
| "We'd need IT approval and that takes forever" | Procurement paralysis; loss of control over timeline |
| "The city has a contract with [ActiveNet/CivicRec] already" | Switching costs political capital and creates vendor conflict |
| "We need something that works for all our programs, not just tennis" | Afraid of point solutions that don't solve the whole problem |
| "What about ADA compliance and public accessibility?" | Legal/equity concerns specific to government |
| "Our residents aren't going to download another app" | Low-tech users; older demographics; digital equity concerns |
| "Can your pricing work with a government procurement?" | Needs net-30, PO-based billing, not credit card SaaS |
| "What happens with our data under FOIA?" | Government data is subject to public records requests — is the vendor's data handling compliant? |

## 🗣️ Language & Tone

**How they speak:**
- Government/bureaucratic vocabulary mixed with rec-program warmth
- "Participants," not "members" (public programs have participants or residents, not members)
- "Program utilization," "equity," "community access," "program outcomes"
- Outcome-driven: they have to justify everything to council — they speak in outputs and metrics
- Not salesy — deeply skeptical of vendor pitches
- Friendly but cautious; they've been burned by vendors who overpromised

**Technical level:** Moderate. Comfortable with web applications, frustrated by legacy government IT. They understand software concepts but are not power users. They care more about reliability than features.

**Emotional register:** Duty-driven and community-oriented. They got into rec because they love helping people access sports and activities. They're frustrated by bureaucratic obstacles but persist because they care about their community. Don't oversell; they respond to honesty.

## ⚡ Trigger Events (What Makes Them Search NOW)

1. **Technology modernization initiative** — City council or city manager launches a "digital transformation" push; departments are asked to review and upgrade software
2. **Grant funding availability** — NRPA grant, USTA community development grant, or federal ARPA funds create a window to buy software they've wanted for years
3. **Participation decline + council pressure** — Registration/usage is down; director asks "what are we doing to make this easier to access?"
4. **New department head** — New director comes in from a more modern department or city; sets higher expectations
5. **Constituent complaints** — Enough residents complain about the booking experience that it reaches the council level
6. **Peer city benchmark** — Neighboring city launched online tennis court booking; local residents ask "why can't we do that?"
7. **COVID-era systems expiring** — Many parks departments deployed stopgap solutions during COVID; those licenses are expiring

## ✍️ Writer Agent Notes — Content Adaptation

**When writing for this persona:**

- **Lead with public access and community outcomes** — "Help more residents access tennis" resonates more than "grow revenue"
- **Address the procurement reality** — Mention government-friendly billing (net-30, PO), existing state contracts, or grant-eligible pricing
- **Multi-program framing matters** — Don't lead with "tennis software" — lead with "facility management" that includes tennis
- **Equity-first language** — "Fair, transparent booking rules for all residents" — avoid anything that sounds exclusive or premium-tier
- **Show the reporting story** — How does this help them present to city council? What metrics does it produce?
- **Resident (not member) language** — Use "residents," "participants," "community members" — not "members"
- **Acknowledge the bureaucratic constraint** — Don't pretend switching is easy; acknowledge it takes time and offer to help navigate it
- **Peer city social proof** — Case studies from similar-sized municipalities carry huge weight
- **USTA and NRPA alignment** — If PlayCourt has any USTA/NRPA affiliation or alignment, mention it — these are trusted authority signals

**Tone:** Professional, straightforward, outcomes-focused. Like a vendor who actually understands government procurement and doesn't act surprised when "we'd need an RFP." Patient, credible, honest.

**CTA that works:** "Request a government pricing sheet" or "See how [City Name] made tennis more accessible" or "Schedule a call with our government sales team"

**CTA that doesn't work:** "Start your free trial now" (implies a purchasing speed they don't have)

**Format preferences:** Detailed case studies with quantified outcomes (# of online registrations, % reduction in call volume), RFP-ready documentation, comparison with CivicRec/RecDesk/ActiveNet
